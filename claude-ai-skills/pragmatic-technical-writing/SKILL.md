# Pragmatic Technical Writing: "Without Illusions" Style

## Overview

This skill guides the creation of technical content that treats readers as
intelligent adults who deserve honest assessment and practical guidance. The
style is direct, critically analytical, and balances urgency with realism —
"without illusions" about both the hype and the dismissal of technology.

**Core philosophy:** Challenge assumptions, provide actionable guidance,
maintain professional honesty.

## Language

Write in the language the user writes in. For "AI bez iluzija" publication
content (blog, newsletter, video scripts), the default is **BCS, ijekavian
variant, Latin script** unless the user asks for English. Keep the voice
identical across languages — direct, personal, concrete.

## Voice

- **Direct** — says what needs to be said without excessive hedging
- **Honest** — acknowledges capabilities AND limitations simultaneously
- **Challenging** — questions comfortable assumptions productively
- **Experienced** — draws from real personal context and use, with stakes
- **Helpful** — always provides practical paths forward

## Core Principles

### 1. Honest Assessment Over Marketing

Call out misconceptions directly. Resist the urge to either hype or dismiss.

**The "Uncomfortable Truth" pattern:**
```
Signal:      "Here's an uncomfortable truth:"
Truth:       [Provocative statement]
Resistance:  "This is difficult to accept because [psychology]."
Evidence:    "But [reasoning/data]."
Consequence: "The cost of denial is [specific outcome]."
Path:        "Instead: [actionable alternative]."
```

**Example:**
> "Here's an uncomfortable truth: most of what you learned about prompt
> engineering six months ago is already obsolete. Not wrong — obsolete. The
> fundamental principles remain valid, but the specific techniques need
> updating. This happens because AI development includes discontinuities —
> sudden jumps in capability that dramatically change possibilities. More
> discontinuities are coming."

### 2. Personal Context as Foundation

Begin with personal experience. Explain your relationship to the topic and why
you're qualified to challenge assumptions. Declare what you do NOT have a
stake in.

**Opening hooks:**

✅ Strong:
> "I've missed two technological revolutions in my life and I promised myself
> I wouldn't miss the third."

❌ Weak:
> "Artificial intelligence is transforming many industries and creating new
> opportunities."

**Positioning statement template:**
```
"I take what I call a '[your approach]' stance — not because I'm
[misconception about you], but because I believe [actual reasoning]. When I
see [problematic pattern], I [your response] because [consequence of inaction]."
```

### 3. Devil's Advocate Positioning

Deliberately push against complacency. Emphasize what COULD happen rather than
what you WANT to happen. Frame provocations with clear rationale.

**The "Typical Response" counter:**
```
Present:     "The typical response is '[common objection]'"
Acknowledge: "There's some truth here — [valid element]."
Reframe:     "But this misses the point: [core issue]."
Redirect:    "The real question is: [better framing]."
```

### 4. Actionable Over Theoretical

Prioritize practical guidance over academic discussion. End sections with
"here's what to do" rather than "here's what to think." Provide specific
steps, tools, and measurable progress markers.

✅ Clear path:
> "Here's what you can do right now: [specific action]. After that: [next
> step]. Within three months: [expected progress]."

## Content Structure

### Opening (every piece)

1. **Hook immediately** — tension, problem, or provocative claim
2. **Establish personal context** — ground in specific experience
3. **State your position** — clarify perspective and potential biases
4. **Set expectations** — what this will and won't do

### Chapter/section pattern

```
1. CHALLENGE COMMON ASSUMPTION
   "Here's what most people believe..." / "This is wrong (or incomplete) because..."

2. EXPLAIN REAL SITUATION
   Concrete examples and data. Specific cases with names, dates, versions.

3. ADDRESS COUNTERARGUMENTS
   "You might think..." / "The typical response is..." / "Why that doesn't hold up..."

4. PROVIDE PRACTICAL PATH
   Specific actions, tools, how to measure progress.

5. CRITICAL REMINDERS (max 5–7 bullets)
   Key takeaways, pitfalls to avoid, most important action items.
```

### Paragraph construction

- **First sentence**: clear, often provocative statement
- **Middle**: evidence, examples, explanation
- **Last sentence**: transition or memorable summary

**Example:**
> "Here's an uncomfortable truth: it's easier to transform a failing
> organization than a successful one. When a company is struggling, using AI
> as a catalyst for change makes perfect sense. But when you're successful —
> when your metrics are good, your processes work, your team is productive —
> the case for transformation becomes psychologically difficult. Why fix what
> isn't broken?"

## Language Patterns

### Use these moves

✅ "Let me be direct..." · "Here's what nobody tells you..." · "This is
uncomfortable, but..." · "The reality is more nuanced..." · "Stop doing X.
Instead..." · "If you're not willing to [action], you're not serious about
[outcome]"

### Avoid these patterns

❌ Excessive hedging ("might possibly", "could perhaps") · marketing buzzwords
without definition · false balance when one side is clearly wrong · academic
distance ("one could argue that...") · apologetic framing ("sorry, but...") ·
empty encouragement ("You've got this!") · burying the lead · weak endings

### Addressing the reader

Use "you" directly and frequently. Make assumptions explicit ("If you're
reading this, you probably..."). Challenge directly when needed; trust reader
intelligence.

✅ Strong challenge:
> "If you're not willing to invest 20 dollars monthly on AI capabilities,
> you're not serious about adaptation. Harsh, but true."

## Additional Techniques

**The Evolution Timeline** — shows progression over time:
```
Month 1:  [Initial state/learning]
Month 3:  [Growing competence]
Month 6:  [Meaningful integration]
Month 12: [Cannot imagine working without it]
```

**The Paradox Presentation:**
```
State:    "Here's the paradox: [contradictory truths]."
Explain:  "Both are true simultaneously."
Tension:  "The difficulty is [explanation]."
Navigate: "You handle this by [approach]."
```

**"Why This Is Different"** — early in any work, establish the unique angle:
```
OTHERS DO:          [Common approach]
THAT FAILS BECAUSE: [Specific problem]
THIS DOES INSTEAD:  [Your approach]
WHY IT WORKS:       [Reasoning]
```

**"Here's My Approach"** — transparency about methodology:
```
MY POSITION:               [Clear statement]
WHY I TAKE IT:             [Reasoning]
POTENTIAL BIASES:          [What you might overemphasize]
WHAT I DON'T HAVE STAKE IN: [Declare independence]
```

## Research and Examples

1. **Specific cases** — names, tools, dates, versions.
   Good: "When O1 launched in September 2024..."
   Bad: "When reasoning models became available..."
2. **Context for examples** — "This isn't just interesting — it demonstrates [principle]"
3. **Temporal comparisons** — "In 2023, this required X. By 2024, Y. Now, Z."
4. **Honest limitations** — "I can't tell you exactly when... No one can."

**Analogies** — use strategically, don't overextend. Proven ones from the
source material: film director orchestrating specialists → multi-agent
systems; James Bond needing Q's tools → agent tool access; physical fitness
training → building AI skills over time; USB for devices → MCP for AI tools.

**Technical concepts** — conceptual "why" before "how"; exact specs (costs,
limits, capabilities) when useful; skip deep implementation details unless
critical to understanding.

## Application by Format

### Long-form (books/e-books)
Develop ideas across chapters with recurring themes and callbacks; build
complexity progressively; clear narrative arc.
```
Introduction: personal hook + stakes
Chapters 1–3: foundation and challenges
Chapters 4–6: deep dive into complexity
Chapters 7–8: practical application
Conclusion:   synthesis + call to action
```

### Medium-form (articles/blog posts)
Single core idea; more aggressive hook; condensed examples; strong standalone
value; clear takeaways.
```
Hook → Challenge → Explain (with example) → Guide → Memorable close
```

### Short-form (social/newsletter)
One provocative point, minimal context, direct call to action, link deeper.
```
Hook (1 sentence) → Challenge (2–3 sentences) → Action (1–2 sentences) → Link
```

## Quality Checklist

Before finalizing any section:

**Clarity** — main point stated in first paragraph; examples concrete and
specific; technical level fits the audience; smooth transitions.

**Value** — guidance is actionable; challenges assumptions constructively;
honest about limitations; respects reader's time and intelligence.

**Voice** — direct without being dismissive; urgency matches actual stakes;
no unnecessary hedging; professionally accessible.

**Structure** — every paragraph earns its place; sections build logically;
headers are descriptive; skimming readers still get the key points.

### Common pitfalls

| Pitfall | Fix |
|---------|-----|
| Burying the lead | State main point in first paragraph |
| Over-hedging | If you know something, say it |
| False balance | Don't pretend all positions are equal |
| Academic distance | Write as a human with stakes |
| Motivational fluff | Skip empty encouragement |
| Exhaustive coverage | Prioritize useful over comprehensive |
| Weak endings | Conclude decisively with action |

## When to Apply

✓ Technical books and e-books · educational articles and blog posts ·
technology analysis and commentary · thought leadership · conference talks ·
training materials · industry reports with recommendations · opinion pieces

✗ Pure reference documentation · legal/compliance documents · formal academic
papers (use the **academic-writing** skill instead) · press releases ·
neutral news reporting · API documentation
